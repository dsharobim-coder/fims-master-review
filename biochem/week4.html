1. Lipids: what they are and why they matter

Lipids are a large and diverse group of biological molecules defined less by one repeating structure and more by their strong hydrophobic character. They contain a high proportion of hydrocarbon relative to polar groups, are poorly soluble in water, and are soluble in nonpolar/organic solvents.

Biological examples in your material include fats, waxes, sterols, triglycerides, phospholipids, sphingolipids, eicosanoids, and vitamins A, D, E and K.

Why does hydrophobicity matter? Water is polar, while hydrocarbon regions are nonpolar. This unfavorable interaction with water drives lipid separation and helps amphipathic membrane lipids self-assemble into barriers.

Main functions

  • Fuel and energy storage.
  • Cell-membrane structure.
  • Steroid-hormone signaling.
  • Bile acids for fat digestion.
  • Local signaling molecules such as prostaglandins.
  • Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K.
  • Glycolipids and lipoproteins.

2. Fatty acids

A fatty acid has a polar carboxylic-acid head and a nonpolar hydrocarbon tail, so it is amphipathic.

Saturated

  • Only C–C single bonds.
  • Maximum number of hydrogens.
  • Straighter chains pack closely.
  • Higher melting point; often more solid at room temperature.

Unsaturated

  • One or more C=C bonds.
  • Cis bonds create kinks.
  • Kinks prevent tight packing.
  • Lower melting point and greater fluidity.

Monounsaturated means one double bond; polyunsaturated means more than one. The course notes contrast natural cis double bonds with trans configurations and emphasize the unfavorable health effects associated with trans fats.

Delta vs omega numbering

  • Delta (Δ): count from the carboxyl carbon.
  • Omega (ω): count from the methyl end.
  • Animal cells can make oleic acid with a Δ9 double bond but cannot introduce Δ12 and Δ15 double bonds; therefore linoleic and linolenic acids are essential in the lecture material.

Melting point

Longer chainHigher melting pointMore hydrocarbon contact and stronger packing interactions.

Triacylglycerols

Glycerol has three hydroxyl groups. Three fatty acids attach through ester bonds formed by dehydration reactions, producing a triglyceride or triacylglycerol (TAG).

Why fats are important

  • Subcutaneous fat lies under the skin; visceral fat surrounds internal organs.
  • Adipose tissue stores lipid reserves used for energy when glucose is low, including fasting, stress and exercise.
  • Adipose tissue is also an endocrine organ and produces bioactive peptides called adipokines that influence adipocytes and other metabolic tissues such as liver.

3. Glycerolipids, glycerophospholipids and sphingolipids

Glycerolipids

Fats are fatty acids attached by ester bonds to a glycerol backbone. Triacylglycerols may be fats or oils depending strongly on saturation and chain length.

Glycerophospholipids

They use a glycerol backbone with two fatty acids and a phosphate-containing polar head group. One end is hydrophilic/polar and the tails are hydrophobic/nonpolar, so these lipids are amphipathic and major membrane components.

Sphingolipids

Sphingolipids use a sphingosine backbone instead of glycerol. The Week 4 notes connect sphingomyelin with cholesterol-rich lipid rafts.

4. How membranes form

The professor material identifies glycerophospholipids as the most abundant membrane lipids, followed by sphingolipids, with cholesterol also playing an important role. Each phospholipid has a hydrophilic head and two hydrophobic tails.

Why does a bilayer form spontaneously? In water, hydrophobic tails cluster away from water while charged/polar heads face the aqueous environment. The phospholipids do not need to be covalently bonded to one another; hydrophobic interactions drive self-assembly into large noncovalent structures.
StructureArrangement
MicelleSingle-tail fatty acids can form spheres with heads outside and tails inside.
BilayerTwo-tail phospholipids form two opposing leaflets.
LiposomeA bilayer closes into a spherical vesicle with an aqueous interior.

Liposomes are artificial membrane-like vesicles that can deliver contents through membrane fusion. The professor material connects them to laboratory transfection and lipid-based delivery systems such as mRNA vaccine delivery.

5. Membrane functions and medical importance

  • Divide space into compartments.
  • Provide selective permeability.
  • Restrict easy passage of water-soluble substances such as ions and glucose.
  • Allow lipid-soluble/small nonpolar molecules such as O₂, CO₂ and CO to cross more readily.
  • Transmit signals between the outside and inside of cells.
  • Remain elastic, dynamic and semipermeable.

Passive vs active transport

  • Passive: no cellular energy; movement down a concentration gradient. Facilitated diffusion can use membrane proteins.
  • Active: requires energy to move against a gradient; the notes use Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase as an example.

Medical importance

  • Membrane changes alter molecular, ionic and water content of cells.
  • The professor notes small membrane-structure changes in anesthetic action.
  • Snake-venom toxicity can involve membrane lipids.
  • Membrane-protein changes occur in disorders such as cystic fibrosis.
  • Some epilepsies involve mutations in ion-channel membrane proteins.
  • Parasite survival can involve altered host membrane permeability and fluidity.

6. Lipid movement and membrane fluidity

How phospholipids move

  • Lateral movement: sideways within one leaflet; occurs readily.
  • Rotation/flexing: movement within the same leaflet.
  • Transverse movement (flip-flop): movement from one leaflet to the other; difficult because the polar head would cross the hydrophobic core.
  • Flip-flop generally requires flippase, floppase or scramblase.

Transition temperature (Tm)

Tm is the midpoint at which a membrane shifts between more solid-like and more fluid behavior. Unsaturated fatty acids lower Tm because kinks interfere with packing. The notes use cold-water fish as an example: more unsaturated fatty acids help membranes remain fluid at lower temperatures.

Cholesterol

Cholesterol sits within or near the membrane and is a major regulator of membrane fluidity. In the professor material and practice questions, cholesterol is emphasized as increasing membrane fluidity similarly to unsaturated fatty acids and broadening the useful fluidity range of the membrane.

Practice emphasis: cholesterol is a normal membrane component and also a precursor for many important biological molecules.

7. Short-chain fatty acids, isoprenoids and lipid signaling

SCFAs

The major short-chain fatty acids are acetate, propionate and butyrate, produced mainly when gut bacteria ferment dietary fiber.

  • Support intestinal-barrier integrity.
  • Influence mucus production.
  • Help protect against gut inflammation.
  • Participate in gut-brain-axis communication.
  • The professor material links propionate and butyrate to enzymes implicated in neuropsychiatric disorders including depression, schizophrenia and Alzheimer’s disease.

Isoprenoids and cholesterol

Sterols are built from 5-carbon units called isoprene. Multiple isoprene units combine and the larger molecule cyclizes to form cholesterol.

  • Vitamin A — related to retinol and carotenoids.
  • Vitamin D — cholesterol-derived and involved in calcium regulation.
  • Vitamin E — antioxidant properties.
  • Vitamin K — associated with green plants and gut bacteria.

Eicosanoids

Eicosanoids are signaling molecules made from fatty acids, especially arachidonic acid. Arachidonic acid normally sits in membrane phospholipids. An inflammatory stimulus can activate phospholipase A, releasing arachidonic acid for signaling pathways.

Lipid signaling can regulate proliferation, apoptosis, metabolism and migration. Dysregulation is linked in the professor material to inflammation, cancer, metabolic disease, cardiovascular disease and degenerative disease.

8. Steroid hormones

Cholesterol is the parent/precursor of steroid hormones. They are synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum and secreted on demand rather than stored as preformed hormones.

Why aren't steroid hormones stored like peptide hormones? Steroid hormones and their precursors are lipophilic and can diffuse through membranes. Cells therefore regulate cholesterol availability, cholesterol mobilization, steroidogenic-enzyme activity and expression of steroidogenic enzymes instead of storing large amounts of finished hormone.

DHEA and progesterone are important intermediates in the lecture material.

9. Hormone receptors

Steroid receptor → gene expression

Steroid hormones diffuse through the plasma membrane and bind intracellular receptors. The hormone-receptor complex forms a dimer, enters the nucleus and acts as a transcription factor. It binds specific DNA sequences called hormone response elements (HREs) in gene regulatory regions and alters transcription.

GPCRs

GPCRs are 7-transmembrane receptors. The professor notes emphasize their broad roles in sensory systems, neurotransmission, endocrine/exocrine function, chemotaxis, exocytosis, blood-pressure control, development, cell growth/differentiation, HIV infection and oncogenesis.

Guanylate cyclase and nitric oxide

Membrane-bound guanylate cyclase receptors convert GTP to cGMP after ligand binding. The professor identifies guanylin/uroguanylin and ANF/NPR receptors. A separate soluble intracellular guanylate cyclase is activated by nitric oxide (NO) and also produces cGMP.

Source boundary: your topic sheet asks how calcium and NO relate. The uploaded Week 4 materials clearly support NO → soluble guanylate cyclase → cGMP, but they do not provide a complete calcium mechanism. I have not silently added outside physiology.

Receptor tyrosine kinases

RTKs are high-affinity cell-surface receptors for growth factors, cytokines and hormones. Professor examples include EGFR, FGFR, VEGFR, RET, Eph and DDR. The insulin receptor is a tyrosine kinase. Kinase activity means phosphorylation activity.

RTK sequence: ligand binds → receptor activates/dimerizes → intrinsic tyrosine kinase activates → tyrosines are phosphorylated → downstream proteins activate → cellular response.

JAK-STAT

JAK is a non-receptor tyrosine kinase. Unlike an RTK, the receptor does not carry the same intrinsic kinase activity; JAK is associated with it.

JAK-STAT sequence: ligand/cytokine binds → receptor components come together → JAK activates → STAT is phosphorylated → STATs dimerize → STAT dimer enters nucleus → gene transcription changes.

The professor links JAK-STAT with cytokines, interleukins and growth factors, and with immunity, cell division, cell death/tumor formation, embryonic development, stem-cell maintenance, hematopoiesis and inflammatory responses. Disruption is linked to skin conditions, cancers and immune disorders.

RTK vs JAK shortcut: RTK = receptor itself is the kinase. JAK-linked receptor = a separate JAK kinase is attached to the receptor. Your notes emphasize that JAK-STAT directly carries the signal to transcription through STAT.

10. First and second messengers

Amino-acid-derived and polypeptide hormones are generally not lipid-soluble, so they cannot diffuse through the phospholipid bilayer and must bind cell-surface receptors.

  • First messenger: extracellular hormone/ligand.
  • Second messenger: intracellular signaling molecule generated or activated after receptor binding.

β-adrenergic cAMP pathway

Epinephrine or norepinephrine → β-adrenergic receptor → G protein → adenylyl cyclase → ATP converted to cAMP → cell-specific response.

cAMP is the second messenger and can activate protein kinases that alter target-protein activity. Phosphodiesterase breaks down cAMP and terminates the signal. Therefore, inhibiting phosphodiesterase prolongs cAMP elevation and intracellular signaling.

Cellular responses to membrane-receptor signaling can include changes in membrane permeability, metabolic pathways, protein/enzyme synthesis and hormone release.
