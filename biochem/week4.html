1. What is a lipid?

Your notes describe lipids as non-polymer macromolecules. They do not have repeating subunits of the same type. Instead, lipids are grouped mainly because they contain a high proportion of hydrocarbon and are extremely hydrophobic or amphipathic.

They are poorly soluble in water but soluble in organic solvents. Examples in your materials include fats, oils, waxes, sterols, triglycerides, phospholipids, sphingolipids, eicosanoids and fat-soluble vitamins.

Main biological functions

  • Fuel / energy storage
  • Cell membranes
  • Steroid hormones
  • Bile acids for fat digestion
  • Prostaglandins/local signaling
  • Vitamins A, D, E, K
  • Glycolipids
  • Lipoproteins
WHY hydrophobicity matters: water is polar while hydrocarbon regions are nonpolar. Amphipathic lipids therefore orient their polar regions toward water and hide their nonpolar regions. This explains micelles, bilayers, liposomes and the membrane barrier.

2. Fatty acids: saturation, cis/trans and melting point

A fatty acid has a polar carboxylic-acid head and a nonpolar hydrocarbon tail.

Saturated

  • Only C–C single bonds.
  • Maximum number of hydrogens.
  • Straight chains pack tightly.
  • Examples listed: lauric, myristic, palmitic, stearic, arachidic acid.

Unsaturated

  • One or more C=C bonds.
  • Monounsaturated = one; polyunsaturated = multiple.
  • Cis double bonds make kinks.
  • Kinks prevent tight packing.

Your notes use butter as a saturated-fat example. Oils contain more unsaturated chains, so their kinked tails do not stack as well and they remain liquid more easily.

Your notes contrast cis with trans isomers and discuss partial hydrogenation of oils, margarine, and the unfavorable LDL/HDL effects of trans fats described in lecture.

Melting point: MORE CARBONS → ↑ melting point. MORE C=C DOUBLE BONDS → ↓ melting point.

3. Delta vs omega numbering — HIGH YIELD

  • Delta: carbon 1 = carboxyl carbon; count inward.
  • Omega: carbon 1 = terminal methyl carbon.
  • Linolenic acid is discussed as omega-3.
  • Oleic acid has a Δ9 double bond and can be made by animal cells.
  • Animal cells cannot make the Δ12 and Δ15 bonds needed for linoleic and linolenic acids → these are essential fatty acids.
WHY the delta position matters: enzyme capability depends on where the double bond must be introduced relative to the carboxyl end. Your lecture emphasizes that animal cells cannot introduce Δ12/Δ15.

4. Triglycerides / triacylglycerols

A triglyceride is glycerol + 3 fatty acids. Glycerol has three –OH groups. A fatty acid attaches to each hydroxyl by dehydration, creating ester bonds.

GLYCEROL + 3 FATTY ACIDS → ester bonds → TRIACYLGLYCEROL (TAG)

Your notes distinguish fats as TAGs solid at room temperature and oils as TAGs liquid at room temperature. Ester bonds must be cleaved to release fatty acids for cellular metabolism.

Why stored fat matters

  • Subcutaneous fat = beneath skin.
  • Visceral fat = around internal organs.
  • Adipose tissue stores lipid reserves used when energy is needed, especially when glucose is low/fasting.
  • Adipose is also an endocrine organ producing adipokines that influence adipocytes and metabolic organs such as liver.

5. Glycerophospholipids and sphingolipids

Glycerophospholipids / phosphoglycerides

They use a glycerol backbone, two fatty acids, phosphate and an R/head group. The phosphate/head end is charged or polar and hydrophilic; the fatty-acid end is hydrophobic. This makes them amphipathic and major bilayer components.

Sphingolipids

They use a sphingosine backbone. A ceramide contains a long-chain fatty acid plus sphingosine. The R group determines the final molecule.

6. Membrane composition + why bilayers self-assemble

Your notes list phospholipids as forming the bilayer, glycolipids in anchoring/recognition, and cholesterol + sphingomyelin in lipid rafts. The bilayer is described as about 5 nm thick.

WHY automatic? Hydrophobic tails cluster away from water while charged/polar heads face water. No enzyme is needed and the individual phospholipids are not covalently bonded to one another.

Liposomes are artificial membrane-like vesicles. Their interior can hold a substance and they can deliver material into cells through membrane fusion.

7. Membrane functions + medical importance

  • Separates outside from inside and creates compartments.
  • Provides selective permeability.
  • Restricts easy passage of water-soluble substances such as ions and glucose.
  • Small lipid-soluble/nonpolar gases such as O₂, CO₂ and CO cross more easily.
  • Supports transport systems and cell signaling.
  • Is elastic, dynamic and semipermeable.
Passive transportActive transport
No cellular energy; high → low concentration. Facilitated diffusion may use a membrane protein.Energy required; movement against gradient. Notes use Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase.

Medical importance in your materials

  • Membrane changes alter molecular, ionic and water content.
  • Anesthetics can cause small changes in membrane structure.
  • Snake venom can act on membrane lipids.
  • Membrane-protein alterations occur in cystic fibrosis.
  • Ion-channel mutations contribute to epilepsies.
  • Professor material also connects membrane permeability/fluidity with parasite survival.

8. Fluidity + lipid movement

Transition temperature (Tm) is the midpoint between more solid-like and more fluid membrane behavior. Unsaturated fatty acids lower melting point/Tm. Your notes use cold-water fish: more unsaturated fatty acids help membranes stay fluid at low temperature.

Movement

  • Lateral: sideways within one leaflet; easy.
  • Rotation/flexing: within same leaflet.
  • Transverse / flip-flop: one leaflet → the other; difficult.

Flip-flop generally requires flippase, floppase or scramblase.

Cholesterol

Cholesterol sits within/near the bilayer and regulates membrane fluidity. Your practice-answer sheet specifically says it increases membrane fluidity similarly to unsaturated fatty acids. It is also an important precursor for biologically important molecules.

9. SCFAs + gut-brain axis

Main SCFAs: acetate, propionate and butyrate. Gut bacteria produce them by fermenting dietary fiber.

  • Maintain intestinal-barrier integrity.
  • Influence mucus production.
  • Protect against gut inflammation.
  • Can act locally or influence other organs.
  • Participate in gut–brain-axis communication.

Your notes specifically connect propionate/butyrate with proteins/enzymes implicated in neuropsychiatric disorders including depression, schizophrenia and Alzheimer disease.

10. Isoprenoids → sterols → cholesterol

Sterols are built from 5-carbon isoprene units. Your detailed notes name dimethylallyl pyrophosphate and isopentenyl pyrophosphate as activated 5-C building blocks. Six isoprenoid units contribute to squalene, which folds/cyclizes toward a sterol. In animals, the first sterol named is lanosterol.

Your notes call plant sterols phytosterols and animal sterols zoosterols. Cholesterol is needed for membranes and as a precursor, despite its association with atherosclerosis.

Fat-soluble vitamin connections

  • A: retinol/carotenoids.
  • D: cholesterol-derived; calcium regulation.
  • E: antioxidant properties.
  • K: green plants and gut bacteria.

11. Eicosanoids — arachidonic acid branch point

Arachidonic acid normally sits in membrane phospholipids. An inflammatory stimulus activates phospholipase A, which releases it.

Clinical link: aspirin/NSAIDs block COX. Your notes emphasize arachidonic acid as a branch point whose product depends on which enzyme acts.

Lipid signals regulate proliferation, apoptosis, metabolism and migration. Dysregulation is linked in the lecture to inflammation, cancer, metabolic disease, cardiovascular disease and degenerative disease.

12. Steroid hormones

Steroid hormones are cholesterol-derived, synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum and secreted on demand. DHEA and progesterone are important intermediates.

  • Glucocorticoids: cortisol; immune/inflammatory processes.
  • Mineralocorticoids: water/salt balance and kidney retention.
  • Androgens: testosterone; male sexual characteristics.
  • Estrogens: estradiol. Notes emphasize the aromatic ring and aromatase, and mention anti-aromatase treatment in estrogen-responsive tumors.
  • Progestins: progesterone; reproductive/pregnancy role and steroid intermediate.
WHY steroids are not stored: they are hydrophobic/lipophilic and diffuse through membranes. Cells regulate cholesterol availability/mobilization, steroidogenic-enzyme activity and expression of those enzymes instead of storing large amounts of finished hormone.

Clinical implication from the slide: if a steroidogenic enzyme is absent/inactive, upstream precursors may accumulate and diffuse into blood.

14. Steroid receptor → HRE → transcription

Steroid hormones diffuse through the plasma membrane and bind intracellular protein receptors. Hormone–receptor complexes form a dimer and act in the nucleus as transcription factors.

An HRE (hormone response element) is a specific DNA sequence in a gene regulatory region. Your notes call it a “landing site” for the receptor dimer.

Gene-expression pathway #1: steroid → intracellular receptor → dimer → HRE → altered transcription.

15. GPCR + first/second messengers

GPCRs cross the membrane 7 times. Ligand binds outside → receptor changes → G protein activates inside → intracellular signaling.

  • First messenger: extracellular hormone/ligand.
  • Second messenger: intracellular relay generated/activated after receptor binding.

Amino-acid-derived and polypeptide hormones are generally not lipid-soluble, so they bind cell-surface receptors rather than diffusing through the bilayer.

Phosphodiesterase (PDE) breaks down cAMP and terminates the signal. Therefore PDE inhibition causes prolonged cAMP elevation. Your notes also state that PDE acts on cyclic AMP/GMP and emphasize the need to turn signals off.

16. Guanylate cyclase + nitric oxide

Guanylate cyclase converts GTP → cGMP. The professor distinguishes membrane-bound GC receptors from soluble intracellular guanylate cyclase, which is activated by nitric oxide (NO).

NO → soluble guanylate cyclase → GTP → cGMP → intracellular response

Slides also list membrane GC receptor systems such as guanylin/uroguanylin and ANF/NPR.

Source boundary: your topic sheet asks how calcium and NO relate. The uploaded Week 4 materials clearly support NO → soluble GC → cGMP, but they do not give a complete calcium mechanism. I have not silently added outside material.

17. Receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)

RTKs are membrane receptors for signals including growth factors, cytokines and hormones. Professor examples include EGFR, FGFR, VEGFR, RET, Eph and DDR. Your notes emphasize the insulin receptor.

ligand → receptor activation/dimerization → intrinsic kinase → tyrosine phosphorylation → downstream proteins → response

18. JAK–STAT vs RTK

JAK = Janus kinase, a non-receptor tyrosine kinase. The receptor does not contain intrinsic kinase activity in the same way an RTK does. JAK proteins are associated with it.

STAT = Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription. Your notes associate JAK–STAT with cytokines/interleukins, immunity, cell division/death, tumor formation, development, stem-cell maintenance, hematopoiesis and inflammation.

Gene-expression pathway #2: JAK → STAT → nucleus → altered transcription.
RTK vs JAK: RTK = receptor itself is the kinase. JAK-linked receptor = a separate JAK kinase is associated with the receptor.
