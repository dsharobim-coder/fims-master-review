1. Requirements of genetic material

Mendel's hereditary factors implied that genetic material must carry information, be transmitted, be replicated, and have enough variation to explain biological diversity. Because chromosomes contained both protein and DNA, protein was initially favored by many investigators.

3. Avery–MacLeod–McCarty

Extracts from smooth bacteria were treated with enzymes that selectively destroyed macromolecules:

  • Protease destroyed protein.
  • RNase destroyed RNA.
  • DNase destroyed DNA.

Transformation persisted after protease or RNase treatment but was lost after DNase treatment. Therefore DNA was the transforming principle.

Experimental logic: only destroying DNA eliminated transfer of the inherited phenotype.

4. Hershey–Chase

T2 bacteriophage contains DNA inside a protein capsid. Hershey and Chase labeled protein with radioactive sulfur and DNA with radioactive phosphorus. After infection, most sulfur stayed in the supernatant with phage coats, while most phosphorus entered E. coli and later appeared in progeny phage.

Protein coat stays outside • DNA enters bacterium • DNA directs production of new virus

5. Nucleosides and nucleotides

A nucleotide contains a 5-carbon sugar + nitrogenous base + phosphate. A nucleoside is just sugar + base.

BASE + SUGAR = NUCLEOSIDE   |   BASE + SUGAR + PHOSPHATE = NUCLEOTIDE

AdenineAdenosine

Adenine + ribose = adenosine. Add one, two or three phosphates to make AMP, ADP or ATP. Adenine + deoxyribose = deoxyadenosine.

6. DNA vs RNA

FeatureDNARNA
SugarDeoxyribose; lacks 2′-OHRibose; has 2′-OH
BasesA, T, G, CA, U, G, C
Typical structureDouble-stranded helixUsually single-stranded

The backbone is linked by phosphodiester bonds. Hydrogen bonds stabilize complementary base pairs: A–T and G–C.

7. Determining DNA structure

Franklin and Wilkins

X-ray diffraction showed DNA was helical, contained more than one strand and had about 10 base pairs per turn.

Chargaff

Across species, DNA composition obeyed A = T and G = C.

Watson and Crick

They used model building and integrated the available biochemical and diffraction evidence into a two-stranded double-helical model with complementary base pairing.

8. Forms of DNA

FormFeatures
B-DNAMost common; right-handed; about 10–10.5 bp/turn; 0.34 nm between bases; base pairs roughly perpendicular to helix axis.
A-DNAMore compressed, more bases per turn, shorter; can occur in RNA–DNA hybrids or enzyme-associated DNA.
Z-DNALeft-handed, transient, associated with GC-rich regions and can be favored by methylation.

9. Major and minor grooves

Because the glycosidic bonds holding each base pair are not directly opposite one another, the double helix creates unequal spaces called the major groove and minor groove.

These grooves expose chemical information that proteins can recognize. Transcription factors and enzymes involved in replication, transcription, repair and regulation commonly bind there.

Week 3 Active Recall

What did Griffith show?

A transferable heritable factor could transform rough bacteria into virulent smooth bacteria.

What result identified DNA in Avery–MacLeod–McCarty?

DNase eliminated transformation, whereas RNase and protease did not.

What did Hershey–Chase label?

Protein with radioactive sulfur and DNA with radioactive phosphorus.

Nucleoside vs nucleotide?

Nucleoside = base+sugar; nucleotide = base+sugar+phosphate.

Key sugar difference between DNA and RNA?

DNA deoxyribose lacks the 2′-OH present in ribose.

Chargaff's rules?

A=T and G=C.

Which DNA form is left-handed?

Z-DNA.

Why do DNA grooves matter?

They expose base-pair chemical information for protein binding in transcription, replication, repair and regulation.
