1. What defines life?

  • Composed of cells: the cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. Organisms may be unicellular or multicellular.
  • Require energy: ATP powers active transport, movement, biosynthesis, chromosome movement, cilia/flagella and maintenance of ion gradients.
  • Reproduce: asexual reproduction includes bacterial binary fission, mitosis in single-celled eukaryotes and budding; sexual reproduction uses meiosis, gametes, fertilization and recombination.
  • Display heredity: DNA stores genetic information and is copied before division so daughter cells receive genetic information.
  • Respond to stimuli: cells detect changes in the internal or external environment and produce a response.
  • Evolve and adapt: populations change in inherited traits/allele frequencies across generations; adaptations are heritable traits that improve survival or reproduction.
  • Maintain homeostasis: internal conditions such as pH, temperature and glucose are kept within viable ranges, usually by negative feedback.
Exam distinction: an individual organism does not genetically “evolve” during its lifetime. Evolution occurs in populations over generations.

2. Cell theory + Pasteur

Schleiden, Schwann and Virchow established the classic principles:

  1. Every living organism is composed of one or more cells.
  2. The cell is the smallest unit having the properties of life.
  3. Cells are produced only by growth and division of pre-existing cells.

Pasteur's swan-neck flask experiment

Pasteur heated nutrient broth to sterilize it. With the curved neck intact, air still entered but airborne microorganisms were trapped in the bend, so the broth stayed clear. When the neck was removed, microbes reached the broth and it became cloudy. This supported biogenesis: new microbial cells came from existing microbial cells.

Why the shape mattered: a sealed flask would also block air, allowing critics to argue that air was necessary for life. The swan neck allowed air in while excluding contaminants.

Stain categories

  • Morphological stains: reveal cell size, shape and organization. Examples include methylene blue and India ink.
  • Differential stains: distinguish organisms/structures. Gram stain is the key example.
  • Acid-fast stains: detect mycolic-acid-rich bacterial walls, as in Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
  • Fluorescent stains: fluorochromes can stain directly or be attached to antibodies.

4. Antibodies and immunofluorescence

Antibodies (immunoglobulins, Ig) are proteins produced by activated B cells/plasma cells. They bind specifically to an antigen. An antibody contains 2 heavy chains + 2 light chains and has two antigen-binding sites with variable regions.

Direct immunofluorescenceIndirect immunofluorescence
A fluorescently labeled antibody binds directly to the antigen. Faster, fewer steps, usually less signal amplification.An unlabeled primary antibody binds antigen; a labeled secondary binds the primary. Multiple secondaries can amplify the signal.
Memory: Direct = one labeled antibody. Indirect = primary antibody + labeled secondary antibody.

5. Prokaryotes: organization, shape and arrangement

Prokaryotes include Bacteria and Archaea. They have no membrane-bound nucleus and lack the membrane-bound organelles of eukaryotic cells. DNA is concentrated in a nucleoid. Most bacteria have one main circular, double-stranded chromosome that is extensively folded and compacted. Many also carry smaller circular plasmids that are separate from the chromosome and can carry genes such as antibiotic resistance.

Arrangement depends on division planes and whether daughter cells separate: diplo- = pair, strepto- = chain, staphylo- = cluster, plus tetrads, sarcina packets and streptobacilli.

Phagocytosis

  1. Phagocyte receptors recognize microbial ligands.
  2. The microbe is engulfed into a phagosome.
  3. The phagosome fuses with a lysosome → phagolysosome.
  4. Reactive oxygen/nitrogen species, low pH and hydrolases kill and break down the microbe.
Capsule logic: if the capsule masks the microbial ligands that phagocyte receptors need to bind, engulfment becomes harder.

Biofilms

Biofilms are organized communities of microorganisms attached to a surface and enclosed in a secreted matrix containing polysaccharides, proteins, extracellular DNA and water. They help bacteria persist despite immune responses, drying and chemicals that would otherwise remove them. Dental plaque is the lecture example; accumulated bacterial acids damage enamel and contribute to cavities.

7. Gram stain + bacterial cell walls

8. Peptidoglycan: structure and synthesis

Peptidoglycan (murein) is a mesh-like polymer composed of alternating NAG (N-acetylglucosamine) and NAM (N-acetylmuramic acid). Short peptide chains attach to NAM and form cross-links between neighboring glycan strands.

NAG—NAM—NAG—NAM + peptide cross-links = strong mesh around the bacterium
  1. Build UDP-NAM and add the pentapeptide.
  2. Transfer NAM-pentapeptide to bactoprenol, a membrane lipid carrier.
  3. Add NAG and move the NAG-NAM-pentapeptide across the membrane.
  4. Transglycosylases extend the glycan/sugar backbone.
  5. Transpeptidases cross-link the peptide side chains.
Know cold: transglycosylation builds the sugar chain; transpeptidation makes peptide cross-links.

9. Gram-positive and Gram-negative special features

Teichoic and lipoteichoic acids

Gram-positive bacteria contain negatively charged teichoic polymers. Teichoic acid is anchored to peptidoglycan. Lipoteichoic acid is anchored to the cell membrane. The slides connect them to Gram-positive viability, movement of positively charged substances through the wall and adherence to surfaces.

LPS

The Gram-negative outer membrane contains lipopolysaccharide. Its three major regions are Lipid A, the core polysaccharide and the variable O antigen. Lipid A is the endotoxin component. Large systemic LPS exposure can trigger cytokine release, endothelial activation, vasodilation and hypotension, capillary leakage, fever, clotting abnormalities and septic shock.

Porins and resistance

Porins are channels in the Gram-negative outer membrane. They regulate entry and exit of small molecules, including some antibiotics. Mutations that change porin number, size, shape or charge can reduce antibiotic uptake. The slides also show ATP-binding cassette transporters across the inner membrane.

10. Flagella, ribosomes, DNA and growth

A bacterial flagellum contains a filament made of flagellin, a hook and a basal body. Arrangements include monotrichous (one), lophotrichous (tuft at one end) and peritrichous (distributed around the cell). Run-and-tumble changes in flagellar rotation allow chemotaxis.

Ribosomes: bacterial 70S = 30S + 50S; eukaryotic cytoplasmic 80S = 40S + 60S. S refers to the Svedberg sedimentation coefficient, not ordinary arithmetic size.

Information flow: DNA → transcription → mRNA → translation → protein.

Binary fission: chromosome replicates → cell elongates → DNA copies separate → septum forms → cell divides into two daughter cells. Generation time is the time required for a cell to divide into two.

1 → 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → exponential growth

Week 1 Active Recall

State the 3 principles of cell theory.

All organisms are made of cells; the cell is the smallest unit with properties of life; cells arise from pre-existing cells.

Why did Pasteur's swan-neck broth remain sterile?

Air could enter, but dust and microorganisms were trapped in the curved neck and could not reach the broth.

Direct vs indirect immunofluorescence?

Direct uses a labeled antibody that binds antigen; indirect uses an unlabeled primary plus a labeled secondary.

What do strepto-, staphylo- and diplo- mean?

Chain, cluster and pair.

Main capsule functions?

Anti-phagocytosis, attachment, protection from drying and biofilm support.

Gram+ vs Gram−?

Gram+ thick peptidoglycan + teichoic acids, purple; Gram− thin peptidoglycan + outer membrane/LPS/porins, pink-red.

What are NAG and NAM?

Alternating sugar subunits of peptidoglycan.

Transglycosylase vs transpeptidase?

Transglycosylase extends glycan chains; transpeptidase cross-links peptide side chains.

Three major LPS parts?

Lipid A, core polysaccharide and O antigen.

What is a porin?

A protein channel in the Gram-negative outer membrane.

70S ribosome subunits?

30S + 50S.

Binary fission sequence?

DNA replication → elongation/separation → septum → two daughter cells.
