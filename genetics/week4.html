Chromatin & Chromosomes

1. Chromosome number

Humans have two sets of 23 chromosomes for a total of 46. Chromosome number varies across species and does not directly correlate with organism complexity.

Gametes = haploid (n) = one set • Somatic cells = diploid (2n) = two sets

2. How chromosome numbers vary

Polyploidy

Duplication of the whole chromosome set produces additional sets. Common in plants, rare in animals; can arise from meiotic/cytokinetic errors or hybrid crosses.

Fission

Chromosome breaks can leave separate chromosome entities, increasing chromosome number. Breaks may also lead to deletion, duplication, inversion and translocation.

Robertsonian fusion

Fusion of two chromosomes/fragments can reduce chromosome number without major gene loss. Human acrocentric chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21 and 22 can participate in clinically important fusions. The lecture uses ancestral fusion to explain human 46 vs chimpanzee 48 chromosomes.

Segmental duplication/deletion

Unequal crossing over can duplicate one segment while deleting the corresponding segment on the partner chromosome.

Break sources in lecture: ionizing radiation, chemicals, infections, replication-fork collapse, unequal crossing over, repair errors, aging and genetic diseases that increase fragility.

3. Karyotyping

A karyotype displays metaphase chromosomes arranged in homologous pairs by size and centromere position. Autosomes are the non-sex chromosomes; sex chromosomes contain sex-determining genes. Typical karyotypes are 46,XX and 46,XY.

The slides note metaphase arrest with colchicine/colcemid or nocodazole, hypotonic KCl swelling, fixation and Giemsa staining for G-banding.

4. Chromosome structure

Chromosomes duplicate during S phase. The two copies of one replicated chromosome are sister chromatids. Chromatids from the maternal and paternal homologs are non-sister chromatids.

Sister chromatids are joined at the centromere. The kinetochore is a protein complex assembled on the centromere and serves as the spindle-microtubule attachment site.

7. Repetitive DNA

  • Unique/nonrepetitive DNA: once or a few times; includes many protein-coding genes; lecture gives ~40% of genome.
  • Moderately repetitive: hundreds to thousands of copies; rRNA genes are an example.
  • Highly repetitive: tens of thousands to millions of copies; often short. Alu sequences are highlighted as a major example.

8. Telomeres

Telomeres are repetitive noncoding DNA at chromosome ends. Human repeats are 5′-TTAGGG-3′, present in many copies.

  • Prevent chromosome ends from attaching to each other.
  • Shorten with repeated replication/cell division, contributing to replicative senescence.
  • Stem cells and germline cells can maintain telomere length using telomerase.

9. DNA packaging into the nucleus

  1. DNA double helix.
  2. About 146 bp wrap around a histone octamer → nucleosome.
  3. Nucleosomes form “beads on a string,” shortening DNA about 7-fold.
  4. H1 linker histone binds linker DNA and helps adjacent nucleosomes compact.
  5. Nucleosomes associate into a 30-nm fiber; lecture shows solenoid and zigzag models.
  6. 30-nm fibers bind the nuclear matrix/scaffold at matrix/scaffold attachment regions to form radial loop domains of roughly 25,000–200,000 bp.
  7. Loops compact into euchromatin or heterochromatin and then into the highly condensed metaphase chromosome.

10. Nucleosome structure

The nucleosome core is an octamer containing 2 copies each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. Positively charged/basic histone residues interact with negatively charged DNA phosphates. H1 is the linker histone and is not part of the core octamer.

Core octamer: 2×(H2A + H2B + H3 + H4). Linker = H1.

11. Chromosome territories and chromatin states

Radial loops help organize chromosomes into discrete chromosome territories in the nucleus, supporting efficient gene regulation, DNA replication and chromosome separation.

EuchromatinHeterochromatin
Less condensed, radial loops more evident, capable of transcription; much interphase DNA is in this state.Tightly compacted, generally transcriptionally inactive.

Before mitosis/meiosis, chromosomes condense dramatically; by metaphase replicated chromosomes are highly compact.

13. X-inactivation

Females have two X chromosomes but largely use one active copy per cell. The other is silenced by X-inactivation and becomes a tightly compacted Barr body.

The maternal or paternal X is generally inactivated randomly early in development, and daughter cells inherit the same inactive X. This creates cellular mosaicism.

Calico cat

A female heterozygous for X-linked coat-color alleles develops black and tan patches because different cell clones retain different X chromosomes as active.

Disease connection

X-chromosome aneuploidies may be more tolerated than autosomal aneuploidies because additional X chromosomes undergo substantial inactivation. The lecture specifically discusses Triple X (XXX) and Klinefelter syndrome (XXY).

Week 4 Rapid Review

  • Humans: n=23, 2n=46.
  • Number changes: polyploidy, fission, Robertsonian fusion, duplication/deletion.
  • Karyotype = metaphase chromosomes organized in pairs.
  • Sister chromatids = replicated copies; kinetochore attaches spindle at centromere.
  • G-band = Giemsa/A-T-rich pattern; C-band = constitutive heterochromatin.
  • Telomere repeat = TTAGGG.
  • Nucleosome core = 2×H2A/H2B/H3/H4; H1 is linker.
  • DNA → nucleosome → 30-nm fiber → radial loops → condensed chromosome.
  • Euchromatin open/transcription-capable; heterochromatin compact/inactive.
  • Condensin compacts; cohesin holds sisters.
  • X-inactivation → Barr body → mosaicism.

Week 4 Active Recall

Human haploid and diploid numbers?

n=23 and 2n=46.

What mechanisms alter chromosome number?

Polyploidy, fission, Robertsonian fusion/translocation and segmental duplication/deletion.

Why use metaphase for karyotyping?

Chromosomes are maximally condensed and individually visible.

What is a kinetochore?

A protein complex assembled on the centromere for spindle attachment.

G-band vs C-band?

Giemsa/A-T-rich standard pattern vs constitutive heterochromatin.

Nucleosome core composition?

Two copies each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4, wrapped by ~146 bp DNA.

H1 role?

Linker histone that helps compact adjacent nucleosomes.

Euchromatin vs heterochromatin?

Less condensed/transcription-capable vs tightly condensed/generally inactive.

Condensin vs cohesin?

Condensin compacts chromosomes; cohesin holds sister chromatids together.

What is a Barr body?

A highly compacted inactive X chromosome.
