Sessions 1–2 — Basics of the Cell

Cell theory

Schleiden, Schwann and Virchow established three principles: every living organism consists of one or more cells; the cell is the smallest unit with the properties of life; and cells arise only from growth/division of pre-existing cells. Pasteur's sterile-broth experiment supported the last principle by showing microbes did not spontaneously appear when contamination was prevented.

Bacterial shapes and arrangements

Know the major morphologies: cocci (spherical), bacilli (rods), and curved/spiral forms. Arrangement reflects how cells remain attached after division: pairs (diplo-), chains (strepto-) and clusters (staphylo-) are classic patterns.

Surface structures

  • Pili/fimbriae: surface projections used for attachment; pili interactions can contribute to aggregates/biofilms.
  • Capsule: secreted polysaccharide or protein coat. It can block phagocyte receptor access, reducing engulfment. Encapsulated colonies often look smooth and capsules may appear as halos with staining.
  • Biofilm: bacterial community embedded in a secreted matrix/slime layer. It promotes persistence despite immune/chemical removal; dental plaque is a key example.
  • Flagellum: bacterial motility structure composed of flagellin; rotation propels the cell.
Clinical link: bacterial capsules can resist phagocytosis. Gram-negative LPS can drive a powerful systemic inflammatory response and septic shock.

Peptidoglycan is a mesh of alternating NAG (N-acetylglucosamine) and NAM (N-acetylmuramic acid). Peptide side chains attach to NAM and are cross-linked.

NAG—NAM—NAG—NAM + peptide cross-links = strong bacterial wall

Synthesis: UDP-NAM-pentapeptide is built; bactoprenol carries NAG-NAM-pentapeptide across the membrane; transglycosylases extend the glycan chain; transpeptidases form peptide cross-links.

Teichoic acid is anchored in peptidoglycan; lipoteichoic acid is anchored in the cell membrane. In the professor material they are negatively charged Gram-positive polymers involved in viability, movement of positively charged substances and adherence.

LPS is a Gram-negative outer-membrane component. Porins regulate entry/exit through that outer membrane; structural changes can reduce antibiotic uptake and contribute to resistance.

Don't mix these up: Gram-positive = thick peptidoglycan + teichoic acids. Gram-negative = thin peptidoglycan + outer membrane + LPS + porins.

Nucleoside vs nucleotide

BASE + SUGAR = NUCLEOSIDE    |    BASE + SUGAR + PHOSPHATE(S) = NUCLEOTIDE

DNA vs RNA

DNARNA
Deoxyribose; A,T,G,C; usually double-stranded; long-term genetic storageRibose; A,U,G,C; usually single-stranded; mRNA, rRNA and tRNA roles

DNA phosphates make the molecule negatively charged. B-DNA is the common right-handed form (~10–10.5 bp/turn). A-DNA is more compressed and can occur in RNA–DNA hybrids or enzyme-associated DNA. Z-DNA is a transient left-handed form associated with GC-rich regions and can occur with methylation.

Major and minor grooves arise because glycosidic bonds are not directly opposite one another. These grooves expose chemical information used as binding sites by proteins involved in replication, transcription, repair and regulation.

Chromosome vocabulary

  • Chromatin: DNA + protein.
  • Haploid (n): one chromosome set, as in gametes. Diploid (2n): two sets, as in human somatic cells.
  • Sister chromatids: duplicated copies of one chromosome after S phase.
  • Centromere: chromosome region joining sister chromatids.
  • Kinetochore: protein structure assembled on the centromere; spindle microtubule attachment site.
  • Telomere: repetitive noncoding DNA at chromosome ends; human repeat = TTAGGG.
  • Autosomes: non-sex chromosomes; sex chromosomes contain sex-determining genes.

Chromosome number can change through polyploidy, chromosome fission, Robertsonian fusion/translocation, and segmental duplication/deletion. The lecture frames these as mechanisms that can contribute to species variation/speciation and disease.

Karyotype: metaphase chromosomes arranged in homologous pairs by size and centromere position. Professor examples: 46,XX female; 46,XY male.

DNA packaging — from helix to chromosome

DNA → nucleosomes (“beads on a string”) → H1/linker compaction → 30-nm fiber → radial loop domains attached to nuclear matrix → euchromatin/heterochromatin → condensed metaphase chromosome

A nucleosome packages DNA around histone proteins and shortens DNA length about 7-fold in the professor deck. H1 linker histone binds DNA between nucleosomes and helps compact adjacent nucleosomes. 30-nm fibers further compact DNA and interact with the nuclear matrix to form radial loop domains; the lecture gives ~25,000–200,000 bp per loop.

EuchromatinHeterochromatin
Less condensed; radial loops evident; capable of transcription; most chromosomal DNA during interphase.Tightly compacted; transcriptionally inactive.

Condensin is an ATP-dependent SMC complex that compacts individual chromatids as cells prepare for division. Cohesin promotes binding between sister chromatids; it remains after S phase and is ultimately removed to permit chromatid separation.

Easy exam distinction: CONDENSIN = CONDENSE. COHESIN = keeps sister chromatids COHESIVE/together.

X-inactivation

Because normal human gene dosage is compatible with only one active X chromosome, one X in female cells is transcriptionally silenced. The inactive X becomes tightly compacted into a Barr body. Daughter cells preserve which parental X was inactivated, creating cellular mosaicism.

Sessions 5–6 — DNA Replication

Semiconservative replication

Each parental DNA strand serves as a template for a new complementary strand, producing two double helices that each contain one old + one new strand.

Meselson–Stahl: bacteria were first grown in heavy ¹⁵N, then shifted to light ¹⁴N. After one generation, DNA was hybrid heavy/light. After two, there were hybrid and light/light molecules. This pattern supports semiconservative—not conservative or dispersive—replication.

Core rule: DNA polymerase READS/MOVES along template 3′→5′ and SYNTHESIZES the new strand 5′→3′.

Bacterial initiation at oriC

E. coli has a single oriC and replication proceeds bidirectionally. The origin contains DnaA boxes, an AT-rich/DUE region that is easier to separate because A–T has two hydrogen bonds, and GATC methylation sites used in regulation.

DnaA/DiaA/IHF bind oriC → AT-rich DNA opens → DnaC loads DnaB helicase → helicase unwinds → topoisomerase relieves torsion → SSB stabilizes single strands → primase lays RNA primer → Pol III extends

Primase makes a short RNA primer (~10–12 nt in the deck). DNA polymerase cannot start a strand from nothing; it adds nucleotides to an existing 3′-OH. DNA polymerase III is the main bacterial replicative polymerase. DNA polymerase I removes RNA primers and replaces them with DNA. DNA ligase seals remaining phosphodiester-bond gaps.

Leading vs lagging + trombone model

FORK MOVEMENT → Template: 3′ ========================= 5′ New: 5′ ------------------------→ 3′ LEADING (continuous) Template: 5′ ========================= 3′ New: ←---- ←---- ←---- LAGGING (Okazaki fragments) each fragment is STILL made 5′→3′

The leading strand is synthesized continuously in the direction of fork movement. The lagging strand is synthesized discontinuously as Okazaki fragments, each requiring a primer.

In the trombone/looping model, helicase, primase and Pol III move as a coordinated replication complex. The lagging-strand template loops so both polymerases can physically travel with the fork while both new strands are chemically synthesized 5′→3′. As an Okazaki fragment finishes, the loop releases and reforms for the next fragment.

Major trap: “lagging” does NOT mean DNA is synthesized 3′→5′. DNA synthesis is always 5′→3′. It is the template that is read 3′→5′.

Proofreading

Only correct base pairs fit properly in the polymerase active site. When a wrong nucleotide is incorporated, polymerase proofreading can remove it and then add the correct nucleotide.

Regulation and termination of bacterial replication

DnaA regulation: DnaA–ATP has high affinity for DnaA boxes and promotes origin opening. ATP hydrolysis produces DnaA–ADP, which has lower affinity. Sufficient DnaA–ATP must accumulate before another initiation.

GATC/Dam regulation: Dam (DNA adenine methyltransferase) methylates adenines in GATC sequences at oriC. The professor deck emphasizes methyladenines aiding recruitment of replication machinery. Immediately after replication, the new strand contains unmethylated adenines, helping prevent immediate re-initiation until methylation status is restored.

Termination: ter sequences T1/T2 lie opposite oriC and bind Tus. They stop replication forks approaching from the specified directions so forks meet in the termination region; ligase completes covalent joining.

ChromosomesUsually circularLarge linear chromosomes

Helicase initiationDnaB loaded with DnaCORC marks origins; Cdc6/Cdt1 recruit MCM2–7; MCM + Cdc6 + Cdt1 forms pre-RC

In G1, ORC is associated with origins. Cdc6 + Cdt1 recruit MCM2–7 helicase to form a licensed pre-replication complex. CDK2–cyclin E and Cdc7–Dbf4 phosphorylation promotes origin firing/replisome assembly; MCM with GINS and Cdc45 participates in unwinding.

Pol α + primase makes an RNA primer (~10 nt) followed by a short DNA stretch (~20–30 nt). Pol δ and Pol ε continue replication. RNase H and FEN1 remove RNA primers/flaps and DNA ligase joins the remaining gaps.

End-replication problem + telomerase

Linear chromosome ends create a problem because DNA polymerases synthesize only 5′→3′ and require a pre-existing primer/3′-OH. Once the terminal RNA primer is removed, there is no upstream site from which conventional polymerase can fill the extreme end, so chromosomes would progressively shorten.

Telomeres protect chromosome ends and contain repeated TTAGGG sequences plus a 3′ overhang. Telomerase, discovered by Carol Greider and Elizabeth Blackburn, contains RNA and protein components. Its RNA serves as a template while telomerase reverse transcriptase extends the 3′ end with telomeric repeats; conventional DNA synthesis can then make the complementary strand.

🧠 Active Recall — answer before opening

What are the 3 principles of cell theory?

All organisms contain one or more cells; the cell is the smallest unit with properties of life; cells arise from pre-existing cells.

Gram-positive vs Gram-negative wall?

Gram+: thick exposed peptidoglycan, teichoic/lipoteichoic acids, no outer membrane, purple. Gram−: thin peptidoglycan in periplasm, outer membrane with LPS and porins, pink/red.

What makes peptidoglycan?

Alternating NAG and NAM sugar chains with peptide side chains/cross-links attached to NAM.

Rough ER vs smooth ER vs Golgi?

Rough ER makes/processes secreted and membrane/lysosomal proteins; smooth ER makes lipids/steroids, detoxifies and stores Ca²⁺; Golgi modifies, sorts and ships ER cargo.

What did Griffith show?

A factor from heat-killed smooth pneumococci could transform live rough bacteria into virulent smooth bacteria: transformation.

What did Avery–MacLeod–McCarty establish?

DNA was the transforming principle; DNase abolished transformation.

What did Hershey–Chase establish?

Phage DNA, rather than protein, entered bacteria and carried the information needed for progeny phage.

Nucleoside vs nucleotide?

Nucleoside = nitrogenous base + sugar. Nucleotide = nucleoside + phosphate(s).

Why do major/minor grooves matter?

They expose DNA chemical information and provide binding sites for proteins involved in replication, transcription, repair and regulation.

Euchromatin vs heterochromatin?

Euchromatin is less condensed and transcription-capable; heterochromatin is tightly compacted and transcriptionally inactive.

Condensin vs cohesin?

Condensin compacts chromatids/chromosomes; cohesin holds sister chromatids together until separation.

What is a Barr body?

The tightly compacted, inactive X chromosome.

What did Meselson–Stahl prove?

DNA replication is semiconservative: each daughter duplex contains one parental and one newly synthesized strand.

Which direction does DNA polymerase move/read vs synthesize?

It reads/moves along the template 3′→5′ and synthesizes new DNA 5′→3′.

Why are Okazaki fragments necessary?

Because both strands must be synthesized 5′→3′ while the templates are antiparallel; the lagging strand is therefore made discontinuously opposite fork movement.

What does the trombone model explain?

The lagging template loops so leading- and lagging-strand polymerases can move with the same replisome/fork while both synthesize 5′→3′.

Primase, Pol III, Pol I, ligase?

Primase makes RNA primers; Pol III performs main bacterial DNA synthesis; Pol I removes RNA primers and replaces them with DNA; ligase seals phosphodiester gaps.

How does DnaA regulate bacterial initiation?

DnaA–ATP binds DnaA boxes and promotes origin opening; hydrolysis to DnaA–ADP lowers affinity, helping prevent repeated initiation until DnaA–ATP accumulates again.

What does Dam do at GATC?

DNA adenine methyltransferase methylates adenine in GATC sequences at oriC; methylation status participates in control of initiation.

What terminates bacterial replication?

Tus protein bound to ter sequences T1/T2 arrests approaching forks in the termination region.

ORC, Cdc6/Cdt1 and MCM?

ORC marks eukaryotic origins; Cdc6/Cdt1 recruit MCM2–7 helicase to create a licensed pre-replication complex.

Pol α vs δ/ε?

Pol α works with primase to make the RNA primer and short DNA segment; Pol δ/ε extend nuclear DNA synthesis.

Why are telomeres needed?

Conventional polymerases cannot fully replicate linear chromosome ends after terminal primer removal, so telomeres provide expendable repeat DNA and telomerase extends the 3′ end.
